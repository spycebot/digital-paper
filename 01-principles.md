# digital-paper Design Principles

Created 29 OCT 2026

Updated 06 NOV 2026


The logic of digital-paper flows as follows:
(1) Old computer manuals are cool
(2) There are circuits drawn on paper that actually work: functional paper circuits using copper tape and graphite pencil
(3) What if the ink on the page could  be manipulated like pixels on a screen


When computer manuals were created in the middle decades of the 20th century, a great deal of design intention did **not** go into them. They were utilitarian artefacts intended to get information about computers into the minds of early enthusiasts and professionals. They were not intended to be objects of beauty in and of themselves. The notable exception to this are IBM manuals which strictly conform to stipulated IBM design aesthetics.

Nonetheless, as the early era of digital computers has closed, the documentation that was left behind, when taken as a whole, does in effect form a kind of retro or antique aesthetic for a bygone age. It was an age of progress and innovation. It was an age when the computers being discussed were the most advanced machines (so it was reckoned) humanity had ever built. Now we live in a time when everything related to computers has advanced: machines are faster, software systems write their own code and are at the threshold of recursive self improvement, and the Internet itself has made printed computer matter effectively obsolete. We have the luxury of looking back at those decades as nascent, somehow quaint, possibly even naive, but undeniably the forebearer of our own increasingly computerised age.

That is the reality, the history. But what about the fantasy, the alternative, the timeline that diverged? **digital-paper** imagines the world where ink on paper not only transmits electricity and combines simple components into complex circuits, it also imagines the world where ink on paper can be manipulated like pixels on a screen. The ink moves, animates, renders and evidences complex computation somehow buried in the thinnest sheets of papwer.

And from fantasy, or science fiction, may arise a new technical reality. For daring to dream what could be, we open the door to what might be, should we be so bold as to design, and then build.

### Backgrounds

As a rule, backgrounds resemble aged paper. Note the Claude frequently used background colour `#faf9f5`.

Projects such as [sng-safety-equipment-inspection](https://github.com/spycebot/sng-safety-equipment-inspection) have used an aged lined notebook JPEG from The Graphics Fairy [Vintage Notebook Paper](https://thegraphicsfairy.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Notebook-Paper-Sm-2-GraphicsFairy.jpg) . More excellent choices on this page.


### Foregrounds

What is black, and white, and red all over?

In the days before the days of desktop publishing, outside of full colour publications, most publications had a very restricted colour pallet: Certainly black, occasionally red or blue. Therefore the **digital-paper** DLS embraces a very restricted foreground colour pallet: Beginning first with black, and adding others such as red or blue on a conservative, restricted basis.


### Boxes

**digital-paper** favours rectangular boxes with sharp corners. Early computer manuals drew boxes as a meagre attempt at enhanced information design, and did not have the luxury of boxes with rounded edges that are ubiquitous in today's digital and print design.


### Animations and Transitions

(**digital-paper** animations have been rendered by Claude.ai and published to Instagram, but further work needs to be done to develop this domain.)


### Rasterised Images

Rasterised images such as photographs, headshots, and memes, conform to a "a 1980s era dot-matrix printer image, on a background of aged paper."


### Vectorised Images

Diagrams, charts, graphs, and rendered text images conform to "a 1990s era laser printer image, on a background of aged paper."
